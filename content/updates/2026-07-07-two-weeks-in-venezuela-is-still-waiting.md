content_en: "<p>Thirteen days after twin earthquakes devastated Venezuela, the country remains in the grip of a deepening humanitarian catastrophe. The official death toll has reached 2,954, with more than 12,000 injured and over 41,000 people still unaccounted for. Nearly 900 aftershocks have rattled the country since June 24. Satellite analysis has estimated that 58,870 buildings were damaged or destroyed, and up to 6.8 million people are affected.</p><p>The UN has warned that the true toll could take weeks — or months — to fully emerge.</p><p><strong>\"El Topo de La Guaira\" — arrested for helping</strong></p><p>One of the most striking and disturbing stories to emerge this week involves Wilmer Cruz, a volunteer rescuer who earned the nickname \"El Topo de La Guaira\" — The Mole of La Guaira — for crawling through impossibly narrow gaps in collapsed buildings to reach survivors and recover victims. Cruz participated in at least 60 rescue operations in the days following the earthquakes.</p><p>On July 1, Cruz was arrested by Venezuelan authorities. Rights group Provea confirmed his detention, and independent Venezuelan outlets reported that the arrest may have been intended to intimidate residents frustrated by the lack of heavy equipment and government support in the disaster zone. He has since been released, but the message was not lost on those watching.</p><p><strong>A man pulled alive after 120 hours</strong></p><p>On July 2 — eight full days after the earthquakes — a married father of two was pulled alive from the ruins of a collapsed shopping mall in Catia La Mar after a 120-hour rescue operation. Video showed rescuers carrying him out on a stretcher as onlookers applauded. Earlier, an 18-day-old baby and her mother were rescued after more than 30 hours buried under their collapsed apartment building. Rescuers kept the infant alive by sliding water through a straw threaded into a pipe in the rubble.</p><p>These moments of hope are real. But they are becoming rarer.</p><p><strong>The government's faltering response</strong></p><p>Venezuela's interim leader Delcy Rodríguez has angrily defended the government's earthquake response in the face of growing public criticism. An Associated Press investigation titled \"Spotless uniforms, stalled cranes\" documented the gap between official claims and what rescue workers and residents are actually seeing on the ground — an overwhelming reliance on civilian volunteers, inadequate heavy machinery, and a military presence that many describe as more performative than practical.</p><p>The government has established 59 temporary camps and distributed nearly 9,500 tonnes of food and 453,000 litres of water. But with up to 6.8 million people affected, this represents a fraction of what is needed.</p><p><strong>Venezuela's Venezuela captain found dead</strong></p><p>Among the confirmed dead is Willner Rivas, the captain of Venezuela's national football team, found alongside his family in the rubble. His death has added a profound weight to the country's grief, with tributes pouring in from across the football world.</p><p><strong>Messi speaks. The Vatican listens. The world must act.</strong></p><p>Lionel Messi, speaking after Argentina's 3-2 World Cup victory over Cape Verde, sent what he called \"a very big message\" to the Venezuelan people. \"It's very difficult to explain,\" he said, expressing support for those who are suffering, grieving, and searching for missing relatives.</p><p>Venezuelan Cardinals Baltazar Porras and Diego Padrón met privately with Pope Leo XIV at the Vatican to brief him on the crisis. At the World Cup stadium in Mexico City, thousands of fans united in a cry of solidarity before the Mexico vs Ecuador match.</p><p>The world is watching.
